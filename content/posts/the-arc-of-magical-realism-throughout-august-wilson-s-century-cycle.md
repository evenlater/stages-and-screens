I discovered playwright August Wilson, as many must, on a freshman-year college syllabus. My Introduction to Theater class had been assigned Wilson’s masterpiece _Joe Turner’s Come and Gone_, and upon reading it I was blown away by the ostensible magical realism in Wilson’s depiction of the power of both Christian myth and African spirituality in the lives of the African American denizens of a Pittsburgh boarding house in 1911. At last, the day came to discuss the play, and we were asked to move our chairs into a circle so that we could dissect the script in the spirit of orbicular community. When my professor brought up the scene where Herald Loomis flips out in the middle of an African call-and-response dance ritual called the Juba, I was ready. I had been studying Walter Brueggemann’s commentaries on Ezekiel 37 and the Hebrew captivity in Babylon in a concurrent religion class and felt particularly qualified to natter on about Herald Loomis’ vision. We read from the play:

> BYNUM: What you done seen, Herald Loomis? 
>
> LOOMIS: I done seen bones rise up out the water. Rise up and walk across the water. Bones walking on top of the water. 

We took turns around the circle, each student discussing what they had perceived in that dramatic moment. I went last. “It’s a reference to Ezekiel 37,” I blurted out, “in which the prophet describes the Vision of the Valley of Dry Bones. Ezekiel says…” I invoked the Bible:  

> “‘I saw a great many bones on the floor of the valley, bones that were very dry. … I prophesied as I was commanded. And as I was prophesying, there was a noise, a rattling sound, and the bones came together, bone to bone. I looked, and tendons and flesh appeared on them and skin covered them.”

August Wilson, I informed the class, was comparing the African diaspora to the exile of the Hebrews from Israel. Ezekiel’s story was meant to reassure the Jews that they would once again walk, alive, in their homeland. 

For a moment it looked as though the professor was impressed. But then: “You’re all brilliant,” she said. We’re _all_ brilliant? I winced in shattering affront as my rightful “attaboy” for a possibly obvious interpretation was dispersed to everyone else as well. But I smiled in agreement, hooked on Wilson’s plays for life. 

More than 30 years later, I’ve seen all 10 of the plays in August Wilson’s Century Cycle in live theaters and then begun again on seeing them all a second time. I’ve witnessed the full arc of Wilson’s thinking about 100 years of magic, realism, history, conflict, hope, despair, prophecy, punchlines, religion and spirituality. I have stayed most entranced by the moments like Herald Loomis’ vision of the bones, or what the LA Times called “Wilson’s magical realism, where signs and portents are as valid as news reports.” And I’ve been dazzled by the way Wilson keeps us guessing whether the magic in his plays is real or not, as he often leaves an ambiguity that doesn’t often attend the magic in works like Gabriel Garcia Marquez’s _One Hundred Years of Solitude_. In fact, Wilson in interviews expresses a resistance to the acceptance of magic that makes his fascination with the supernatural all the more mysterious and intriguing. This January an excellent chapter on Wilson’s realism was released in a book called _American Narrative Realism_ by David R. Shumway by Cambridge University Press. It argued that what Wilson does is not best described as magical realism. Over 30 years I’ve been cataloguing the moments in my head that best prove or disprove the same thesis, and in recent weeks the question has introduced new thoughts to me on the subject.

This month a pair of plays that have a key position in The Century Cycle are being performed in New York and Chicago. Off-Broadway, director Ruben Santiago-Hudson is launching a new production of _Seven Guitars_ at the Lincoln Center. The show opens to press on November 23 and runs through January 10. And in Chicago, City Lit, the theater company that bills itself as the city’s oldest storefront theater, is performing _King Hedley II_, the sequel to _Seven Guitars_. (In 2024 City Lit won a non-equity Jeff Award for Best Production of a Play, the nonunion edition of Chicago’s Best Play Tony, for _Seven Guitars_. Both that show and _King Hedley_ have been directed by Manny Buckley.) Meanwhile in January, Chicago’s exceptional Tony-winning Court Theatre will follow last season’s Broadway production of _Joe Turner_ with one of its own. That show will see director Ron O.J. Parsons join the ranks of directors who have directed the entire Century Cycle at one theater. All this has put Wilson back on my radar screen, and seeing City Lit’s _King Hedley II_ set me on a new set of contemplations about the master playwright. 

What hit me about the plays after watching Hedley this time was an interpretation of The Century Cycle in its totality. I agree with Shumway that magical realism is not the best way to describe Wilson’s plays. However, the degree to which there is magic in August Wilson’s plays, I realized, is built into the arc and architecture of the whole series. The plays progress from a period of greater spirituality to a period of less literal supernaturalism in a way that presents a kind of “moral” from Wilson on the topic: Redemption is to be found in reconnecting with African heritage, and there are more things in heaven and earth than are dreamt of in the philosophy of the ever-resilient Black church, but in the end every individual must find his own salvation. 

**The arc: a gradual waning of the magical**

Both African and Christian spiritualism are strongest in the plays that depict the earliest decades of the century (_Gem of the Ocean_, _Joe Turner_) and end up hanging by a thread in the plays that show the final decades (_King Hedley II_, _Radio Golf_). I’m not the only one to notice that the spirituality in Wilson’s plays faces new challenges and wanes as his century progresses. “These plays about the first decades of the century are to a degree less realist,” Shumway writes, “not only because of the role of ritual in them, but also because, according to Wilson, they represent a historically distinct period in African American life.” This sentiment underscores, as Shumway puts it, “Wilson’s belief that this aspect of African American life has declined over the course of the century.” 

In fact, the trajectory runs something like this: In _Gem of the Ocean_’s 1904, and in _Joe Turner_’s 1911, spiritual visions assist the characters in finding their own redemption. In _Ma Rainey’s Black Bottom_ in the 1920s, a fierce rejection of Christianity is voiced by a character who ends up in his confusion committing a terrible murder, reinforcing the sense of the dominance of the spiritual from the opposite perspective: the fierce rejection that its very strength engenders. In _Seven Guitars_’ 1940s and _Fences_’ 1950s, African spirituality has largely been replaced by the persistence of Christianity, as we will see. In the 1960s, _Two Trains Running_ introduces us to a character who will come to represent the persistence of memory in the Black community, but she appears otherwise in a vacuum of spirituality. By the 1970s, in _Jitney_, the story is almost entirely told in social realism rather than magical realism. 

Then as Wilson gets to the 1980s, in _King Hedley II_, the spiritual is virtually dead in Wilson’s Pittsburgh community. _Radio Golf_ in the 1990s shows us the consequences of the 1980s on faith. “An anguished sense of the waning power of spirituality in the lives of African Americans in the late 20th century infuses Mr. Wilson’s writing in _King Hedley II_,” wrote Charles Isherwood in The New York Times while reviewing an Off-Broadway revival of that play in 2007. “The death of Aunt Ester, the community’s mystic advisor, referred to throughout this cycle of plays, is its most obvious expression.” 

**Reconnection to spiritual heritage**

Aunt Ester represents the community’s persistent healing connection to their African past. In several of Wilson’s plays starting with _Two Trains Running_, she offers her clients the potential of redemption by her familiarity with the Black community’s spiritual heritage, as she is a spiritual counselor wizened by memory and age. That age would appear to be one of the prime examples of magical realism in Wilson’s plays. In _King Hedley II_ she is referred to as being 366 years old, her life story dating back to the first appearance of African slaves in Virginia in 1619. Is she really that old? The characters in the play variously wonder, debate, and stand on conviction on that point. Shumway for his part lists Aunt Ester’s age as one of two exceptions to the rule that “because Wilson’s plays lack magic, they are not best understood as magical realism.” He says the ghosts that we will see dominate the end of Wilson’s 1930s-set play The Piano Lesson “or a woman of Aunt Ester’s age would fit easily in a magical realist narrative.” 

Interestingly, however, Shumway does not include as proof of his assertion that the plays are not magical realist the detail that Aunt Ester seems to turn out, in _Gem of the Ocean_, not to have been born in 1619 after all. In that play, the next-to-last authored by Wilson and the first in order of the century story, Aunt Ester discloses to her servant Black Mary that she was given the name Ester when she was nine years old by another woman named Ester Tyler. The younger Ester appoints Black Mary as the next Aunt Ester, and the cycle continues. “As a lineal role, revivified in each generation, Aunt Ester sustains threads of collective memory, replicating the principle of royal and papal succession: the head does not die, but rather regenerates with a new face,” writes Barbara Lewis in her essay “Miss Tyler’s Two Bodies: Aunt Ester and the Legacy of Time,” in editor Alan Nadel’s anthology _August Wilson: Completing the Twentieth-Century Cycle_. 

Some critics continue to write, as does Shumway, as though Ester is as old as she says she is. Despite the clarity of the succession scene in _Gem of the Ocean_, perhaps this area of magical realism serves as both one of the areas of ambiguity upon which Wilson’s plays thrive, and simultaneously an instance in which Wilson discourages the ultimate reliance of his people on magic and therefore superstition. “Of course, no one is going to live to be 285 years old,” Wilson says, citing her alleged age as of _Gem of the Ocean_. “She represents any old person in the community that keeps its traditions alive. She’s not mystical or magical. She can’t help you find your way to redemption. You have to find it.” Thus, Aunt Ester is also an embodiment of Wilson’s recurrent thesis that one must find one’s own redemption. This is seen in each of Ester’s attempts to assist characters, including her suggestion that characters throw money in the river. That, she confesses hilariously in Gem of the Ocean, is just to give her clients something to do. 

It would seem, then, that it’s Black Mary who dies in _King Hedley II_, which takes nothing away from the fact that Hedley is the bleakest of all the plays. The story is set in the Reagan era, during which governmental support for poor communities was slashed, cocaine became cheaper and wiped out Black communities, and homicide rates therein skyrocketed. (These factors are nicely established by Sandra G. Shannon’s discussion of _King Hedley II_ in Nadel’s anthology). Hope of all kinds is thin for the Hill District in Pittsburgh (wherein all the plays but one take place) in 1985 (when Hedley takes place). With Aunt Ester gone, the only conduit for spirituality left in this bleaker than usual Wilson story is the penniless prophet Stool Pigeon, who informs the cast in the middle of Act One of Aunt Ester’s death. He begins and ends the play with prophetic monologues that may be the ravings of a madman or may be the wisdom of a prophet. Brandon Boler’s Stool Pigeon in the City Lit production tended, to my eye, more to the former, which contributed to my musings about the death of the spiritual in the play.

Still, in a way Hedley also concludes with the most hopeful moment in terms of the reality of the spiritual. It’s a subtle one, so it does little to break the sense of the immense challenges faced by the magical in 1985. The play begins and ends with the meow of a cat. In between the two meows Aunt Ester’s cat is represented to have died. Stool Pigeon buries the cat in Hedley’s garden, hoping (bizarrely) that it will be splashed with sacrificial blood and then rise again. This resurrection Stool Pigeon equates with the resurrection of Aunt Ester: “If she ain’t used up her nine lives, Aunt Ester’s coming back.” At least one critic, Soyica Diggs Colbert in Nadel's anthology, sees the final meow as evidence that Aunt Ester has been reincarnated. I’m not sure that’s the way the prophecy works. But whatever the debate about the nature of Aunt Ester’s resurrection, the cat as a symbol presents another glittering example of Wilson’s beautiful ambiguity. 

Still, the impact of the tough 1980s on the belief of the characters in the magical is profound. When the curtain rises on the next play in the series, _Radio Golf_, which is set in 1997, hope in spirituality is dead in at least the most successful of the residents of the Hill District. Mame Wilks, the publicist and wife of mayoral candidate Harmond Wilks, rejects the religious magic suggested in an early scene by the character of Old Joe.  

> “OLD JOE: The people put up their money. God walked over and stuck his hand in the pot and stirred it around. Told one of the fellows, ‘Now you do it.’ Man walked over and tried to stick his hand in the pot and had to draw it back. The pot was boiling! If you had thrown some beans in there you could have cooked your supper. He asked the people if they wondered how he could do that. He said, "I am He." He didn't say he was God. He just say, "I am He." But who else could he have been. Made a pot boil without fire. Can you do it? If you can't do it you ain't got nothing to say.
>
> MAME: You say the people put up their money? They were betting against God? Well, tell me, did God walk out the door with the money? If God walked out the door with the money he wasn't nothing but a Negro from Mississippi with some dry ice.”

As the last play in the series, both in terms of the order in which Wilson wrote them and the decade it depicts, this lack of faith in the spiritual is fitting. It seems to echo Wilson’s own degree of skepticism about magic. By the end of the play, the heritage of Aunt Ester is restored and revived, as her lineage in the community is learned and her house appears to perhaps be spared demolition. But Aunt Ester appears only in the sense that the Black Mary turns out to be survived by relatives who didn't know they were related, and that a black cat keeps leaping on the cars of the wealthier Black people. Maybe it is a literal reincarnation after all. But this is but a faint hint of potential magic and contrasts mightily with the visions of the early plays. That does not reduce the power of Wilson’s evocation of a spiritual heritage — if anything it makes it even more powerful. 

**The Black church is a rock to the ages**

Still, this degree of spirituality is different from what we saw earlier in the series. Wilson’s plays are not, however it may seem at times, wholly negative about Christianity. “The Christian church is the one institution that has enabled us and contributed tremendously to our survival,” Wilson told Sandra G. Shannon and Dana A. Williams in _Conversations with August Wilson_. “It’s the most stable organization in the black community and has been for years.” This is seen in the hope we the audience glean from Seven Guitars, which while a tragedy is considerably cheerier than its sequel. “There is a communal joy about the production that reflects the sense of community Mr. Wilson dramatizes,” wrote Vincent Canby in The New York Times upon the opening of the first Broadway production of Seven Guitars, which is set in the 1940s. This is true in many of Wilson’s plays. In 2001 in The New Yorker Wilson told John Lahr that “community is the most valuable thing that you have in African-American culture. The individual good is always subverted to the good of the community.” There is community in Hedley, too, but it is fractured and tortured by the tragic events that conclude _Seven Guitars_ —the consequences of which violence in some ways are deferred to the later play. 

It isn’t just communal joy, though, that lightens the proceedings in _Seven Guitars_. It’s also Christian belief, and therefore the continued impact of the church on the survival of the Black people through the challenges of the diaspora and beyond. The play begins and ends with a communal gathering that follows the funeral of the play’s lead, Floyd. In each scene, first and last, the characters propose supernatural visions that reveal Floyd, for all his moral failings, has gone to heaven. 

> “VERA: Did you see them angels out there at the cemetery? 
>
> RED CARTER: Uh-oh, Vera done saw some angels. 
>
>  CANEWELL: I saw them out there too. They look like they was with the funeral home, only they wasn’t. Wasn’t that them, Vera? Had on black hats.” 

In the end, Floyd is described as having risen out of his coffin as the angels carried it. 

As they do with Aunt Ester’s age, the characters wrestle with Christianity. “Ain’t but a hundred and forty-four thousand people going to heaven,” Canewell (who ends up being called Stool Pigeon in the sequel) says at one point. “That’s what it say in Revelations. Say one hundred and forty-four thousand of all the tribes of Israel shall be saved. All the rest gonna be cast into a fiery pit. Next time I’m going to bring my Bible and show you.” By the end of the play, Canewell appears to have decided there is room for more in heaven, however. “He might have gone to heaven now,” Canewell says of another character. “You don’t know. The devil don’t take everybody you think he ought to.” Wilson’s only non-ensemble play, the 1950s family drama _Fences_, also rests its final moment of hope in heaven imagery, as patriarch Troy is pictured as heaven-bound. But in that play the heavenly vision comes through the mentally impaired character of Gabriel, so hope is arguably more clouded in that tragedy. In any case, the “magic” in the middle decades of the 20th century is mainly conveyed in terms of heaven and the ongoing strength of the Christian church. That doesn’t mean Wilson endorses Christian theology. But it does underscore his point about the role of the church in Black communities. 

**More things in heaven and earth**

The one area where Wilson endorses the supernatural unambiguously is in _The Piano Lesson_’s depiction of ghosts in the 1930s. As Shumway reports, the character of “SUTTER’S GHOST” even briefly appears in the script in all caps, as though he is one of the dramatis personae. This openness about ghosts too lines up with Wilson’s personal views. In _Conversations with August Wilson_, the playwright is asked whether he believes in ghosts. He answers that while he hasn’t seen any, he believes those who say they have. The powerful conclusion of _The Piano Lesson_, which Wilson wrote hastily on a deadline, would be very different if this were not true. Thus at least one form of magic has allure to Wilson. Shumway explores this in more detail. That the ghosts are a strong a magical element as they are in this 1930s story reinforces the sense that the plays progress towards a lessened spirituality.

**You have to find your own salvation**

In the end, however, the spiritual emphasis even in the early plays is on finding one’s own salvation. The character of Levee voices this sentiment in his stinging rejection of Christianity in _Ma Rainey’s Black Bottom_. Similarly, after Herald Loomis’ vision of the bones rising and gaining life, Loomis rejects the Christian ritual of salvation. 

> “MARTHA: You got to be clean, Herald. You got to be washed with the blood of the lamb. 
>
> LOOMIS: Blood make you clean? You clean with blood? … I don’t need no one to bleed for me! I can bleed for myself.” 

Loomis slashes his own chest and rubs the blood over his face. To explain the philosophy behind moments like this, in interviews Wilson repeatedly cited a statement by one of his greatest influences, the writer Amiri Baraka. He explained:

> “Amiri Baraka has said that when you look in the mirror, you should see your God. If you don't, you have somebody else's God. So, in fact, what you do is worship an image of God which is white, which is the image of the very same people who have oppressed you, who have put you on the slave ships, who have beaten you, and who have forced you to work. The image was a white man. And the image that you were given to worship as a God is the image of a white man. Loomis in Joe Turner rejects that image of Christianity. We're talking about image, not the religion—there's nothing wrong with the religion. It has great principles if it's practiced. But Loomis rejects the idea that he needs someone else to bleed for him."

Wilson said all the other peoples of the world worship a god that looks like them: Whether Jewish, Native American or white, only the Black man worships a god that doesn’t look like himself, he states.

Wilson depicts Loomis’ beautiful vision of bones without much ambiguity as to its spiritual reality: What he sees is at least internally real to Herald. The bones, representing the Africans who died by sea in the Middle Passage while being transported from home to slavery in America, rise from the water as Ezekiel’s bones rose from dust. As do the bones in Ezekiel’s vision, Herald’s bones gather flesh and walk onto a new land. This element of “magic,” echoed in _Gem of the Ocean_’s very similar City of Bones odyssey, begins the series with a spiritual power in the 1900s and 1910s that wanes by the 1970s in _Jitney_, which while it pits some of its characters’ religious fervor against economic displacement, is as was stated earlier almost entirely told in terms of strict realism. And in the 1980s, as we have seen, the spiritual seems almost to die with Aunt Ester. 

At the beginning of this essay, I posited that the plays spell out a kind of thesis on religion and spirituality: Redemption is to be found in reconnecting with African heritage, and there are more things in heaven and earth than are dreamt of in the philosophy of an ever-resilient Black church, but in the end every individual must find his own salvation. Those points are all conveyed by Wilson in my favorite of his plays, _Joe Turner_, as though he didn’t need a full career to birth so rich and robust a depiction of the spiritual over a century. The experience of the African Juba unearths a redemptive experience for Loomis that is effectuated by a reconnection to spiritual heritage. The bony vision he experiences comes distinctly from the imagery of the Christian church, which remains a powerful rock to a community in peril—but Wilson uses those images to rebut Christian theology. Ghosts may be real, and the expungement of them healing as in _The Piano Lesson_. But in the end salvation lies not in magic or superstition but in the individual him or herself. 

Still, as Wilson told Lahr, the individual good is always subverted to the good of the community. Because when it comes down to it, we’re all brilliant.
